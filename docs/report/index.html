Stairs to Release: a simulation training and validation pipeline for industrial humanoid locomotion

How we trained a Unitree G1 to climb staircases in simulation, measured it with statistics rather than demos, and built a release gate that withheld the policy when the evidence fell short.

Authors Lahiru · Ahmed · Roth  ·  Industrial partner context: SKF, machine elements and production

Abstract

The question is how to make humanoid training in an industrial setting easier and more efficient. We argue that the bottleneck is not the learning algorithm but everything around it: simulation fidelity, evaluation that can be trusted, and a release process. We built a full pipeline for two locomotion skills on the Unitree G1: commanded step length on flat ground, and vision-based stair climbing. We used reinforcement learning (Brax PPO on MuJoCo Playground), a terrain curriculum, warm starts, domain randomisation and a height scan from a simulated head depth camera.

Main result: a simulation training and validation framework demonstrated substantial locomotion improvements and correctly withheld release, because perception and latency requirements remained unmet.

Over 14 stair versions, falls on staircases kept out of training went from 28 of 32 (v1) to 1 of 96 (v14) when the policy reads the true terrain height from the simulator. With the rendered head camera, the sensor it would deploy with, v14 crossed 86 of 96 with 8 falls. That is below our 90 % crossing target, and it limits the camera-qualified step height to 6.4 cm against a 10 cm target. With one extra control step of actuation delay (40 ms in total) 17 of 32 runs fell, and with a 5 kg torso payload 4 of 32 fell. v14 did not pass its release gate. "Qualified" in this report means only that a policy passed our stated simulation tests. It is an internal gate, not evidence of conformity to any safety standard, and nothing has run on hardware.

Three limits apply to every number here. The staircases were never trained on, but we used their results to choose checkpoints and plan changes, so they are a validation benchmark, not a final test. In the camera condition the controller still receives exact base velocity and pose from the simulator. Shin and knee collisions with the stairs are not modelled. We trace the largest gains to making the simulator's foot contact and the action range physically right, but each version changed several things at once, so these attributions are not controlled. The data favour a layered design: RL for locomotion, demonstrations for manipulation, and deterministic sequencing for workflows. A language-model supervisor did not measurably change safety and remains an optional experiment. The SKF Skill Studio platform makes runs repeatable: each iteration takes about an hour and US$1–3 of GPU time, and releases are approved by role. Whether it reduces engineering effort, the dominant cost, has not been measured yet. §12 sets one bounded milestone to settle both questions.

§1The problem

Industrial sites were built for people. Stairs, mezzanines, thresholds and platforms are everywhere, and a mobile robot that cannot use them needs ramps, lifts or a redesigned floor. The appeal of a humanoid is that it fits the existing plant. Its weakness is that every new skill today is a research project.

1.1 What makes humanoid skills hard to industrialise

  • Balance is not optional. An arm that stops is safe. A humanoid that stops can fall. Locomotion policies must be robust to things they have not seen: other floors, pushes, payloads, sensor noise and control latency.
  • Data is expensive or unavailable. Nobody can record thousands of demonstrations of a robot falling down stairs. Demonstration-based methods also cannot teach recovery.
  • Results are hard to trust. A policy that looks good in one video can fail on the next staircase. We measured this: changing the commanded step by 4 mm moved a 32-crossing test from 30/32 to 27/32. Small tests measure noise.
  • Simulation lies in specific ways. The training engine and the test engine (and later the real robot) resolve contact differently. A policy will exploit whatever the simulator gets wrong.
  • Compute and tooling are fragile. GPU capacity comes and goes, logs explode, quotas run out, and without lineage nobody can say which checkpoint was tested.

1.2 The task we chose and why

The brief asks for a full pipeline for either walking up stairs or changing step length. We did both, because together they cover the two ends of the difficulty scale:

Step length (flat)

A command-tracking problem. Can the robot walk at a given speed and a given step length, independently? This tests whether the reward and observation design can add a new controllable degree of freedom to an existing gait.

Stair climbing (vision-based)

A perception and robustness problem. The robot must see the terrain (height scan from a head depth camera), place its feet on treads it has never seen, and not fall, including under pushes, low friction, payload and actuation delay.

Layers, not rivals

The seven rows are not all alternatives to each other. Teleoperation is a way to collect data. Imitation learning learns from that data, and RL learns from a reward. Sim-to-real randomisation is a transfer strategy wrapped around either one. Human feedback is a correction channel, and VLA models are a model class that can play several of these roles. A working pipeline takes one choice from each layer, for example teleoperated demonstrations, then imitation learning, then an RL fine-tune with randomisation in simulation. The matrix puts them side by side only to show where each is strongest.

2.1 What the comparison says

Locomotion → RL with sim-to-real hardening

It needs no demonstrations, trains in hours, and is the only method in this project that got the G1 onto stairs. Its weak points are explainability and the reward engineering effort, which this report addresses with evaluation and gates rather than hope.

Manipulation → demonstrations first

Where parts and fixtures are fixed, manual programming is still the answer. Where they vary, teleoperation and imitation learning (ACT or diffusion policies) are the fastest route to a working skill. RL comes in afterwards to fine-tune robustness in simulation.

Workflows → classical sequencing of gated skills

End-to-end learning of a multi-step job has sparse rewards and compounding errors. A state machine or behaviour tree that calls gated, released skills is auditable and matches how plants already run.

Foundation models → optional, above the controller

Our supervisor is a language model that picks a gait mode, and its latency (0.39 s median, 0.59 s p95 in the benchmark, up to 2 s under load) describes only that system. VLA models such as OpenVLA are a different family that predicts robot actions directly, and we did not measure one. In our tests the language supervisor did not measurably change the fall count (§5.8). For a fixed task we keep deterministic logic as the baseline, and language-based mission interpretation stays an experiment.

Where hybrids win

Our own system is already a hybrid, because each layer does what its method is best at. A learned 50 Hz gait handles balance. A classical P-controller holds heading. Code enforces limits: above 25° of tilt it commands zero speed. That is not yet a safe stop, because no balancing or recovery response is defined or tested for it. An optional language-level supervisor reads the mission and picks the gait at 2 Hz. §5.8 shows that, so far, it adds mission awareness but no measurable safety.

§3Our approach

Thesis: an industrial humanoid skill is a released artefact with evidence, not a trained network. Training is cheap. What makes it easier and more efficient is a loop that finds failures fast, fixes their causes in simulation, and releases only what passes a statistical gate on terrain and sensors the policy has never seen.

3.2 Five design decisions

  1. Reinforcement learning in simulation, no demonstrations. Locomotion is where RL is strongest and data is hardest to collect. Thousands of parallel robots produce about 100k steps per second on one GPU.
  2. Start from the closest existing skill. The stairs policy starts from the flat walking policy. New height-scan inputs get zero weights in the first layer, so at step 0 it walks exactly like its parent (checked: maximum action difference 0.0). Each new version starts from the best previous checkpoint, which made iterations take about an hour instead of days.
  3. Curriculum over terrain, not reward hacking. An 8 × 8 grid of staircases from 2 to 16 cm. Each robot moves up a level after it crosses and down after a fall, and most graduates are sent back to the tallest rows, where the falls are.
  4. Train in one engine, test in another. Training runs in MuJoCo Warp/MJX on the GPU. Qualification runs in plain MuJoCo (C) on a CPU, a different contact implementation, which catches policies that exploit one solver. Both engines share the same robot model, collision set and actuator model, so agreement between them says nothing about the hardware (§4.6).
  5. Judge with statistics, release by role. Rank checkpoints on thousands of episodes with 95 % Wilson intervals, qualify finalists on 96+ crossings, and have a person other than the author approve the release.

3.3 Why this should work: the argument

PPO with massive parallelism reliably finds a gait when three conditions hold: the task is reachable (the action space and physics allow the motion), the reward cannot be satisfied by a shortcut, and the training distribution covers what the evaluation asks for. The early stair versions failed on each of these conditions in turn:

  • Reachability. v1–v8 had one contact point per foot and a ±0.5 rad joint-target range, which capped knee torque near 75 Nm when the motor gives 139 Nm.
  • Shortcuts. v3 learned to turn away from hard stairs.
  • Coverage. v5 fitted its stride to one tread depth, and no version before v14 had seen actuation latency.

Our pipeline makes each condition checkable before and after training, so a failure points to its cause instead of just a lower score.

§4Detailed technical implementation

4.1 Inputs, processing and outputs

The system works at three time scales. Each has its own inputs, processing and outputs, and the output of one feeds the next: a training run produces a policy, the policy drives the robot, and the robot's evaluation data decides whether the policy is released.

A · Pipeline level: one training job (about an hour)

Inputs
  • Skill manifest (skill.yaml): task, parameters, stages, gate thresholds
  • Robot model: G1 MJCF (29 joints, masses, joint limits, motor torque limits)
  • Terrain spec: 8 × 8 curriculum grid (2–16 cm rises; 25/29/33 cm treads) and the validation layout (named test in code)
  • Site measurements: friction range, actuation latency, payload, pushes (as randomisation ranges)
  • Parent checkpoint for the warm start (--init-from)
  • Compute target and budget (GPU-hour cap, daily ledger)
Processing
  1. Preflight: engine parity on 5 stair poses, throughput, warm-start identity (max action difference = 0)
  2. Rollout: 8192 simulated G1s step in parallel on the GPU, 20 steps each per iteration, with domain randomisation and sensor noise
  3. Learn: rewards → advantages → PPO update (4 epochs × 32 minibatches); observation normaliser updated from running statistics
  4. Curriculum: each robot promoted or demoted a level at the end of its episode
  5. Checkpoint 20 times per run (every 5–15M steps); evaluation metrics to progress.csv
  6. Screen: every checkpoint × 2048 validation crossings, ranked by falls with Wilson intervals
Outputs
  • params.pkl + 20 ckpt_*.pkl: network weights and observation normaliser
  • progress.csv: reward terms, crossing rate, terrain level, episode length per evaluation
  • rank_true.json, rank_camera.json: per-checkpoint fall rate by step height
  • config.json: the exact environment and PPO settings (lineage)
  • Box stopped; GPU-hours and cost written to the ledger

4.3 Policy and learning algorithm

An asymmetric actor-critic: the actor sees what the robot can sense (IMU, joint positions and velocities, previous action, gait phase, commands, height scan, all with noise). The critic additionally sees privileged state from the simulator. Both are MLPs with 512-256-128 hidden units. PPO hyperparameters are Playground's G1 values: 8192 parallel environments, unroll 20, batch 256 × 32 minibatches, 4 updates per batch, discount 0.97, clip 0.2, entropy 0.005, learning rate 3e-4 from scratch and 1e-4 for fine-tunes (v10 onwards). The control rate is 50 Hz (ctrl_dt = 0.02), and the policy outputs PD joint targets around the default pose.

4.4 Step-length task

The command is (v, ℓ): forward speed in [0.3, 0.9] m/s and step length in [0.15, 0.35] m. Cadence follows from them, f = v / 2ℓ, limited to 0.9–1.8 Hz. The policy gets ℓ and f in its observation and a gait-phase clock running at f. One reward term is added to Playground's G1 locomotion terms. At each touchdown, with Δx the distance of the landing foot ahead of the stance foot:

rstep = exp( −(Δx − ℓ)² / 0.05² )

The touchdown detector needs at least 0.1 s of air time, so contact flicker does not produce fake steps.

4.5 Stairs task

Terrain and curriculum

  • 8 × 8 grid. Each row is a level (2, 4, … 16 cm rise); columns alternate pyramids (walk down from the centre) and pits (climb out).
  • Tread depth per staircase is 25, 29 or 33 cm (v7+), so the stride cannot fit one staircase.
  • A robot is promoted after crossing (more than 1.9 m of progress along its heading) and demoted after a fall or when it covers less than half its commanded distance.
  • Past the top level, 70 % of robots replay the 12–16 cm rows (v12).

Perception

  • 55-point height scan (an 11 × 5 patch from 0.3 m behind to 1.2 m ahead) with ±2 cm noise.
  • Camera scan model (v5-cam, v13+): the error model of a head depth camera (D435i-like, 45° down, 212 × 120 depth, noise growing with distance², 3 % dropout, body pixels removed), fused into a 5 cm elevation map. It includes per-row blind-spot probabilities.
  • Measured camera error against simulator truth on 8 cm stairs: 0.57 cm mean on the walking line and 0.18 cm on the policy's scan patch.

Rewards and terminations

  • Playground G1 terms (velocity tracking, orientation, torques, action rate, feet slip, air time, pose…).
  • Added: a relative base-height cost, lookahead foot clearance, and a feet_edge cost at touchdown only (v4). The per-tick version dominated the return in v3.
  • A fall is tilt above 60° or the pelvis less than 0.45 m above the ground. Turning more than 60° off the target heading also ends the episode as a fall (v5), which closed the "turn away" loophole.

Commands and action space

  • Stairs gait sampled at 1.25–1.6 Hz (v11): short steps of about one per tread.
  • Heading command, converted to a yaw rate from the heading error.
  • Leg action scale 1.0 rad for hip pitch, knee and ankle pitch (v8). The warm start rescales those output weights so the parent's behaviour is preserved.

4.6 Contact fidelity: the fix that mattered most

MuJoCo Warp makes at most one contact between a box-shaped sole and the stairs heightfield. Plain MuJoCo makes 3 to 20. So v1–v8 trained standing on one point per foot and were tested flat-footed. We replaced the sole contact with six spheres per foot (one contact each in both engines), widened friction randomisation to all foot–floor pairs, and enlarged the contact buffers. The next version, v9, went from a qualified height of 3 cm to 9.9 cm on its final single-start test. v9 also trained for 500M steps, so the gain is not attributable to the contact change alone. The preflight now checks parity on five stair poses before every run. Feet across a step edge agree to 0.1 cm between engines. One known gap is left and reported, not gated: a sole lying lengthwise on an edge slides in Warp and holds in MuJoCo C.

What engine agreement does not show

Parity means two implementations of the same model agree. Six spheres per sole made them agree, but whether six spheres represent the real G1 foot is unverified. Warp and native MuJoCo differ in documented ways, and both share the model's assumptions. The biggest gap is that the Playground G1 scene collides only the feet with the terrain: a shin or knee that would strike a real stair nosing passes through it here, so a policy can succeed with motions that would fail on real stairs. Before any claim of hardware potential beyond a research hypothesis, we need full leg–terrain collisions, realistic stair-edge geometry and dynamic contact checks (§12).

4.7 Robustness randomisation (v14)

The first qualification suite showed that every policy since v1, flat walking included, fell within seconds under what we labelled a 20 ms actuation delay. We later found that the CPU evaluator already applies one control step of delay in every condition, so that condition was really 40 ms (§5.5). Playground's recipe applies each action in the same control step. v14 samples a delay of 0 or 1 control steps per episode (50 % of episodes) and floor friction from 0.25–1.0, down from Playground's 0.4–1.0. The changes planned for v15 (70 % delayed episodes, 0–4 kg of torso payload) are now separate one-change arms in the improvement campaign (§8.2). None has been trained yet.

§5Results

All numbers below come from result files in the repository and were produced by the tools in §4.8. Where the sample is small, we say so.

Two caveats that apply to all of §5

Validation, not test. The stair suites were never trained on, but we used their results to pick checkpoints, diagnose failures and decide the next reward, curriculum and physics changes. Repeated adaptation makes them a validation benchmark. Their heights and tread depths also lie inside the training range, so they measure interpolation, not transfer to arbitrary factory staircases.

Evaluator latency. The CPU evaluator's action queue starts one entry too long, so every action is applied one control step (20 ms) late in every condition. "Strict" therefore ran at 20 ms and "delay_20ms" at 40 ms. A GPU re-run with a correct 20 ms delay reproduced the strict results (v14: 2/96 against 1/96; v12: 9/96 against 5/96). The fix changes every historical result and the platform's strict stage, so it is pending. This report relabels the affected results rather than re-running them.

5.1 Baseline first: do we need learning at all? (E5)

Unitree's pretrained G1 policy (an LSTM) takes a speed command and an internal gait clock trained at 0.8 s. We swept 7 commanded speeds (0 to 1.2 m/s, including standing still) × 5 gait periods × 3 seeds with light pushes and sensor noise: 105 runs in 15 s on a laptop CPU, with no falls. Achieved step length for the six moving speeds (cm):

Step lengths from 7 to 40 cm are reachable at zero training cost by re-timing the clock, but speed and step length are coupled. Off the trained period, speed tracking degrades: at 0.4 m/s the achieved speed ranges from 0.36 to 0.48 m/s. At 1.2 m/s with a 1.0 s period the gait breaks down. Precise, independent step-length control needs step length in the task definition. That is what training adds, and E5 is the baseline it must beat.

A lock-step comparison from an identical state shows joint velocities diverging by about 3 rad/s after one control step, so the policy is sensitive to how each engine resolves foot contact. A classical heading-hold loop fixes this, which is how such policies are deployed.

§8How to evaluate this pipeline

A pipeline should be judged on whether its verdicts are right, not on a single policy's score. We evaluate at three levels.

8.1 Policy level: the qualification protocol

  • Validation terrain. Heights (3.0, 4.7, … 15.0 cm) sit between the training levels. Tread depth is 27 cm against 25/29/33 cm in training. No policy is scored on its own training stairs. Because these results steered development, they measure validation performance, and interpolation inside the training range.
  • Final test (to add). A separate suite that stays sealed until the policy and checkpoint are frozen, including staircase families outside the training ranges (nosings, open risers, other tread depths). It is run once and reported whatever the outcome.
  • Several start poses per staircase (3), because single-start results are near-chaotic.
  • Qualified height is the tallest height at which every run at that height and below crossed with no fall, tilt below 25° and pelvis above 0.55 m. It is a floor, not an average.
  • Separate outcome labels: falls, stalls (refusals and timeouts) and heading violations are counted separately (§5.5).
  • Wilson 95 % intervals on every rate. The gate compares the interval's upper bound to the threshold, so a lucky small sample cannot pass.
  • The deployment sensor is required. The camera condition counts toward the envelope.
  • Robot-safety numbers: share of time torque-saturated (v14: 4.6 %, v12: 13 %) and at joint limits.

What the intervals establish, and what they do not

Intervals make small samples honest, but they are not a safety guarantee. v14's 1 fall in 96 has a 95 % upper bound of about 5.7 %, which does not establish a fall probability below 1 %. With the same two-sided Wilson method:

These bounds hold only for the tested distribution. The strict test is 96 fixed scenarios (8 heights × 4 staircases × 3 start poses) in a deterministic simulator. Repeating nearly identical scenarios adds runs, not independent evidence, and says nothing about factory-wide reliability. Choosing the best of many checkpoints, and looking at many conditions, also raises the chance that a good number is luck (§5.6). The final test is how we will protect against that.

8.2 Comparison level: is a change really better?

The improvement campaign (g1pipe.improve, built but not yet run for real) trains one-change arms against a control that is warm-started from the same checkpoint and trained for the same number of steps. The benchmark gives every policy the same 2048 start states per condition. As implemented today, the decision uses a two-proportion z-test per condition on the aggregate fall counts, widened by the seed-to-seed noise measured between two control runs. An arm counts as a win only if an off-target condition improves at z ≥ 2.5, no condition gets worse at z ≥ 2.5, and the gap to the targets shrinks. Using 2.5 rather than 2 accounts for testing seven conditions. Language-model advisors may propose the next experiments, but code checks every knob against a whitelist and makes the keep-or-drop call.

To change: because start states are shared, the benchmark should store per-episode outcomes and compare arms with a paired test (McNemar's test on discordant episodes), which is both correct and more sensitive. Repeated rounds against the same benchmark also need a correction for multiple looks, and any champion should be confirmed on the untouched final test.

10.2 Where the value lands for a manufacturer

Brownfield access

A humanoid that climbs qualified stairs reaches mezzanines, platforms and inspection walkways without ramps or lifts. That is capital avoided for every route the robot can use as built.

Skill cost curve

Warm starts, manifests and automatic gates aim to make each new variant of a supported skill mostly configuration and compute. The measured compute cost is a few dollars per iteration. The dominant cost is engineering hours, which the lessons-as-checks design is meant to reduce. That reduction is not measured yet (§10.4).

Safety evidence

Each release carries a policy card: conditions, intervals, envelope, torque use, lineage, and who approved it. That is input to a risk assessment under ISO 10218, or later under the humanoid standard ISO 25785-1, which is still a draft. Passing our internal gate is not evidence of conformity to either.

Vendor independence

An open stack (MuJoCo, JAX, Brax) running on commodity GPUs, with everything but training on a laptop. The same pipeline can target other humanoids by swapping the model and the manifest.

11.3 Feasibility of the manipulation part

Pick and put is feasible now with demonstrations. Rings on trays and fixtures are rigid parts with known geometry, which is where imitation learning performs best. The risks are part-to-part variation and occlusion by the hand; sim augmentation and a pose check before release address both. Wiping is contact-rich and partly deformable (the cloth). A learned trajectory with a classical force controller is more realistic than end-to-end RL, and coverage can be measured directly. Both are harder for a humanoid than for an arm because the base is not fixed: the locomotion policy must hold a stable stance while the arms work. That is a whole-body control problem we would address with a standing-balance skill trained through this pipeline, with arm motion as a disturbance.

Benefits, limits and readiness

Benefits: no plant changes; one robot type across stairs, handling and cleaning; every skill carries evidence. Limits: humanoid payload and hand dexterity are below an industrial arm's; cycle times will be slower. Safety: fenceless operation near people needs speed and separation monitoring and a certified stop; learned skills sit inside that envelope and never replace it. Readiness: locomotion has passed simulation tests with the true terrain scan but not its release gate; manipulation is at design level, with an arm stand-in in first training.

§12Roadmap and conclusion

We keep the existing training stack and pause platform expansion until one tightly defined milestone is complete:

Reproduce and evaluate camera-based ascent of a fixed five-step staircase, with realistic body collisions and latency, using a frozen policy and a previously untouched test set.

The deliverable is a package: source, the exact configuration, the checkpoint, the sensor assumptions, episode-level outcomes and the gate decision. A team member who did not build it then reproduces it, and we record their effort. That answers both questions this project poses: whether the robot learned the skill, and whether the framework makes that work easier.

Conclusion

In simulation, stair climbing on a humanoid is learnable in hours of GPU time with open tools. We took it from 28 falls in 32 to 1 in 96 on staircases kept out of training, with the simulator's true terrain heights. With the head camera the policy still fell 8 times in 96, and with one extra step of delay it fell in half the runs, so the release gate withheld it. That verdict is the achievement we can defend. The framework found the failures, named their likely causes and refused to release on incomplete evidence. What made the progress possible was not a better algorithm but treating the skill as an engineering artefact: a simulator checked against a second engine, a curriculum, a warm start, targeted randomisation, statistical evaluation and a release gate. Whether this also saves engineering effort is the next thing to measure. Our answer to how to make industrial humanoid training easier and more efficient: make every lesson a check, make every check automatic, and let people approve what the checks cannot see.
